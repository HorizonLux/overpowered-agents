# Claims and disclosures: what to check, and why

Read this before you hand over any plan, calendar or post (Step 6 of `SKILL.md`), and whenever a user asks for something on the refusal list.

**This is general information, not legal advice.** Most rules quoted here come from the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC). Other countries and some fields (health, finance, food, alcohol, children) have their own rules. Point the user to the official text for their market, and to a lawyer when they are unsure. Pages were checked on the date given next to each.

## 1. Proof before it runs

The FTC's guide for small businesses (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business, checked 2026-10-23):
- "The law requires that advertisers have proof before the ad runs."
- "Under the law, advertisers must have proof to back up express and implied claims that consumers take from an ad."

"Implied" matters. "The fastest way to book a repair" is a speed claim even without a number. So check:

| What you find in a draft | What to do |
|---|---|
| A number (customers, savings, time, results) | Keep it only if the user can show where it comes from. Otherwise remove it, or write `[TO CONFIRM: source]` |
| A superlative ("best", "fastest", "#1", "leading") | Remove it unless the user holds the proof. Replace with a plain fact ("fixed price, listed below") |
| "Guaranteed" or a promised result | Remove it unless the user really offers a guarantee; then state its terms next to it |
| A comparison with a named competitor | Keep it only if it rests on the competitor's own current page, with the date checked. Suggest the user has it reviewed |
| A "before and after" | Keep it to what the user really changed and can show |

## 2. Reviews, testimonials and endorsements

The same FTC guide says: "All endorsements must reflect the honest experience or opinion of the endorser."

The FTC's final rule on fake reviews and testimonials, announced on 14 August 2024 (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials, checked 2026-10-23), covers, in the press release's words:
- "reviews and testimonials that misrepresent that they are by someone who does not exist, such as AI-generated fake reviews";
- "providing compensation or other incentives conditioned on the writing of consumer reviews" expressing "a particular sentiment, either positive or negative";
- "certain reviews and testimonials written by company insiders that fail to clearly and conspicuously disclose" their connection;
- "selling or buying fake indicators of social media influence, such as followers or views".

So:
- Never write a review, testimonial or customer quote for the user to publish as if a customer wrote it.
- A real customer's words may be used only with that customer's permission, unchanged in meaning.
- When the user asks customers for reviews, the same request goes to every customer, with no reward tied to what they say. On asking only happy customers, the FTC's Q&A on the rule (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers, November 2024, checked 2026-10-23) says: "The rule does not contain a specific prohibition against such conduct." and "But this practice could violate the FTC Act." Google bans it on its reviews: merchants may not "selectively solicit positive reviews from customers" (Google Maps user-generated content policy, https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114, checked 2026-10-23).
- Staff, family and friends who post about the business must say so.
- No bought followers, likes, views or votes.

## 3. Disclosing a relationship

The FTC's Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, November 2019, checked 2026-10-23):
- "Disclose when you have any financial, employment, personal, or family relationship with a brand."
- "Place it so it's hard to miss."
- "Use simple and clear language."
- "Don't assume that a platform's disclosure tool is good enough" (the sentence goes on to suggest using it together with your own disclosure).
- "If posting from abroad, U.S. law applies if it's reasonably foreseeable that the post will affect U.S. consumers."

The same page says that for a video "the disclosure should be in the video and not just in the description uploaded with the video", and for an image post to "superimpose the disclosure over the picture". It gives "advertisement", "ad" and "sponsored" as clear terms.

In practice: put the disclosure in the post itself, near the start, in plain words such as "Ad", "Sponsored", "I was paid to post this", "They gave me this for free" or "My sister runs this shop". Decide it when the calendar row is written, not at posting time.

## 4. The word "free"

The FTC's guide on "free" offers says the terms, conditions and obligations "should be set forth clearly and conspicuously at the outset of the offer" (https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/guide-concerning-use-word-free-similar-representations, checked 2026-10-22). In the EU, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive lists among practices always considered unfair describing a product as free "if the consumer has to pay anything other than the unavoidable cost of responding to the commercial practice" and delivery (Annex I, point 20, text as adopted: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/annex/I/adopted, checked 2026-10-22; check the current text for your country).

Habit for every draft: the conditions sit right next to the word "free", in the same sentence or the next. If using the free thing still costs the customer something (a subscription it depends on, shipping, a required purchase), say so there.

## 5. AI-made content and AI people

- If an agent drafted a piece and a reader would ask how it was made, say so. Google's question for site owners is: "Is the use of automation, including AI-generation, self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways?" (https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content, last updated 2026-10-05, checked 2026-10-23).
- Never present an AI-generated face, voice or person as a real customer, employee or founder. Label AI images as AI images.
- Never invent an author, a byline, credentials or a headshot.

## 6. Email and messages

This skill's rule: email only people who opted in. It is the skill's own choice, not a statement of what every law requires. For a first sales email to a business, use the outreach skill, which checks the rules by region. For US recipients, follow the FTC's CAN-SPAM guide (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business, checked 2026-10-23): an accurate subject line, a valid physical postal address, and opt-outs honoured within 10 business days. Do not import contacts from elsewhere or buy lists. Other countries have their own laws.

## 7. What the user decides

You flag; the user decides. For each item you could not settle, list it under TO CONFIRM with what would settle it ("a screenshot of the order count", "the customer's written OK", "the competitor's pricing page, dated"). Never mark a claim as checked when you only assumed it.
