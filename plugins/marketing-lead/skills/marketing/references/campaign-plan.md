# Campaign plan: the seven boxes in detail

Read this when you write a campaign plan (Step 3 of `SKILL.md`). It explains what makes each box good, gives a filled example, and shows which parts an agent may draft.

## Campaign plan or marketing plan?

A **marketing plan** covers a year: the market, the competition, prices, overall goals. The US Small Business Administration (SBA) says "Marketing plans should be maintained on an annual basis, at minimum." (https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/marketing-sales, checked 2026-10-23).

A **campaign plan** covers one push with a start and an end: a launch, a seasonal offer, a new service. It borrows the year's thinking and turns it into decisions for the next few weeks. This skill writes campaign plans. If the user has no marketing plan, do not stop; note it under TO CONFIRM as a later job.

## The boxes, and what "good" looks like

| Box | Good | Not good enough | Ask the user when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goal | A number, a result the user can count, a date: "[N] booked tune-ups by [date]", with N chosen by the user | "More awareness", "grow the brand", a number you suggested | The user gives no number or no date |
| Audience | A situation in two or three sentences: what they are trying to do, what they use now, the moment they look for better | An age range and a job title | Nothing comes from real customers: mark it `[TO CONFIRM: talk to N customers]` |
| Message | One sentence a customer could repeat to a friend, plus one reason to believe that the user can show | A slogan, a list of features, a claim with no proof | The reason to believe is missing |
| Offer | What they get, the one action, the conditions, all in one place | Two or three actions competing; conditions in small print | "Free" or a discount appears without conditions |
| Channels | One main channel, up to two supporting ones, and "not this time" | Five channels at once | The user picked a channel because it is popular, not because the audience is there |
| Budget | Money per item, the user's hours, a stop line | One total with no breakdown; hours left out | Any amount is a guess: mark it |
| Measure | The number, where it is read, which day, what would change the plan | "We'll see how it goes" | The user has no way to count the result yet |

Write the goal and audience first. If the goal is missing, every other box drifts.

## Filled example (placeholders, not results)

An imaginary business: a one-person bicycle repair workshop promoting a spring tune-up to people who commute by bike. **Every value in square brackets is a placeholder.** No number here is a result or a benchmark, and the user's plan must not borrow one from it.

```
CAMPAIGN PLAN: Spring tune-up          Dates: [1 March] to [30 April]
1. GOAL      [N] booked tune-ups by [30 April]
2. AUDIENCE  Situation: people who ride to work most days and keep putting off a service
             until something squeaks or slips.
             Today they use or do: wait for a breakdown, or use a chain shop [distance] away.
             The moment they look for better: the first dry week of spring, or a flat on the way in.
             Evidence: [notes from conversations with N customers at the counter]
3. MESSAGE   For people who ride to work, a bike that is ready before the season starts.
             Reason to believe (provable): [the fixed price and the checklist of what is done,
             shown on the booking page]
4. OFFER     They get: the tune-up on the published checklist at [price].
             The one action: book a slot online.
             Conditions: [parts extra, listed with prices]; [booking needed]; [offer ends 30 April]
5. CHANNELS  Main: [the local cycling group where commuters already ask for advice]
             Support: one email to past customers who opted in; a poster at the counter.
             Not this time: paid ads.
6. BUDGET    Money: [poster printing: amount]. My hours: [N per week]. Stop line: [date or amount]
7. MEASURE   Number: bookings from the booking page. Read in: [the booking tool's list].
             When: every [Friday], review on [30 April].
             Stop or change if: [fewer than N bookings by mid-March].
WHO DOES WHAT  Goal, offer, budget: me. Message variants and the poster text: agent drafts, I check.
TO CONFIRM     Parts prices; how many slots I can take per week.
```

Notice the message: "ready before the season starts" is only fair because the checklist and price are on the booking page. If the price could change after the bike comes in, the sentence would have to change too.

## Which parts the agent may draft

| Part | Agent drafts? | The user checks |
|---|---|---|
| Turning rough notes into the seven boxes | Yes, as a first pass | That no "fact" was added |
| Variants of the one-sentence message | Yes, two to five | Picks one; every promise in it is provable |
| The budget box from costs the user listed | Yes, reformatting only | Every amount matches a real quote, invoice or plan |
| A list of numbers, superlatives and comparisons to verify | Yes | Decides what is provable and holds the proof |
| The goal number | No | Only the user knows what is realistic |
| The audience description | Only from the user's notes | That it matches what customers said |
| Spending, publishing, sending | Never | Always the user |

## Measuring: tagged links

Give each channel its own link with `utm_source`, `utm_medium` and `utm_campaign`, all lowercase. Google Analytics' help says "When you add parameters to a URL, you should always use utm_source, utm_medium, and utm_campaign." and "Use lowercase as a standard practice." (https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10917952, checked 2026-10-21). Keep one list of allowed values for the campaign so "Newsletter" and "newsletter" do not split the results.
