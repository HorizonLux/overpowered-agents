# Email outreach rules: United States, United Kingdom, European Union

> **General information, not legal advice.** Rules differ by country and they change. Every quote below was read on the official page linked next to it on 23 October 2026. Re-read the current page before you rely on it, or ask a lawyer.

Read this file during Step 2 and Step 6 of `SKILL.md`. It gives the rule for each region, quoted from the law or the regulator, and what that means for a first email and its follow-ups.

## How to use this file

For every recipient, settle two things first:

1. **Region.** Where is the recipient? (The US, the UK, an EU country, or somewhere else.)
2. **Type.** Is the recipient a company (this includes an employee writing from a company address), or a single person (a sole trader, a freelancer who has not formed a company, some partnerships)?

Then read the matching section. If the sender and the recipient are in different regions, more than one set of rules can apply. Follow every set that may apply, or ask a lawyer which one does. A region this file does not cover is not "allowed": it is unchecked (see "Other countries").

## Summary

|---|---|---|---|

[US-1] | Same CAN-SPAM requirements [US-1] | Honest header and subject; says it is an ad; your physical postal address; how to opt out; opt-outs honoured within 10 business days [US-1] |

The GDPR applies to the named person's data where it applies to you [EU-2][EU-3] | Prior consent, except your own existing customers for similar products [EU-1] | Do not hide the sender; a valid address to stop the emails [EU-1]; say who you are and where you found their details by the first message [EU-2]; the right to object, shown clearly and separately [EU-3] |

Result labels used by the skill (column "Rules check" of the prospect list):

- **OK to email, rules apply**: the region and type allow a first email without prior consent, and every message follows the "always" column.
- **Consent needed**: do not send a cold email. Use another route (see `SKILL.md`, Step 3).
- **Check national rule**: someone at a company in the EU. The user checks that country's rule before sending.
- **Not covered**: any other country. Nothing is sent until the user has checked that country's official rules.

## United States: CAN-SPAM Act

Source: US Federal Trade Commission (FTC), *CAN-SPAM Act: A Compliance Guide for Business* [US-1]. The statute is 15 U.S.C. 7704 [US-2].

- Business to business is covered: "The law makes no exception for business-to-business email." [US-1]
- The FTC's guide lists these requirements, in this order [US-1]:
  - "Don’t use false or misleading header information."
  - "Don’t use deceptive subject lines."
  - "Identify the message as an ad."
  - "Tell recipients where you’re located."
  - "Tell recipients how to opt out of receiving future marketing email from you."
  - "Remember that subscribers and members can opt out of marketing emails, too."
  - "Honor opt-out requests promptly."
  - "Monitor what others are doing on your behalf."
- Saying it is an ad: "you must disclose clearly and conspicuously that your message is an advertisement." [US-1] In a one-to-one sales email, plain words such as "I'm writing to offer…" do this job; the subject line still has to be honest.
- Postal address: "Your message must include your valid physical postal address." [US-1] If the user works from home, ask which address they want in every footer before drafting.
- Opt-out timing: "You must honor a recipient’s opt-out request within 10 business days." [US-1] The statute bars further commercial email "more than 10 business days after the receipt of such request", and asks for "a valid physical postal address of the sender" (fragments) [US-2]. The skill's habit is the same day.
- After an opt-out: "you can’t sell or transfer their email addresses, even in the form of a mailing list." [US-1]
- Tools and helpers: "even if you hire another company to handle your email marketing, you can’t contract away your legal responsibility" [US-1]. Treat every email an agent or tool drafts or sends for the user as the user's own.
- Permission: the FTC's list of requirements does not include getting the recipient's permission before the first email [US-1].

Not covered here: US state laws; whether CAN-SPAM reaches a sender based outside the US; penalty amounts (the FTC page shows one, but it was last edited in January 2024, so read the current page); commercial email sent to mobile-phone addresses, which has a separate rule. If any of these matters to the user, they ask a lawyer.

## United Kingdom: PECR and the UK GDPR

Sources: the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) pages [UK-1][UK-2][UK-4][UK-9], the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations 2003, regulations 2 and 22 [UK-8][UK-3], the UK GDPR, Article 14 [UK-6], and, as a pointer only, the Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002 [UK-7]. Both ICO guidance pages carry this notice: "Due to changes made by the Data (Use and Access) Act, this guidance is under review and may be subject to change." [UK-1][UK-2] Re-check them before each campaign.

**The law.** Regulation 22 "applies to the transmission of unsolicited communications" "by means of electronic mail to individual subscribers." [UK-3] For those subscribers, nobody may send unsolicited marketing email "unless the recipient of the electronic mail has previously notified the sender that he consents" (fragment) [UK-3]. The version in force was amended on 5 February 2026; the new paragraph (3A) concerns charities only [UK-3].

**Companies.**
- "The PECR rule on direct marketing by electronic mail does not apply to corporate subscribers." [UK-1]
- An employee's work address counts as the company's: "the email address or telephone number of an employee at a corporate body would constitute a corporate subscriber" [UK-1].
- You must still not hide who you are and must "give a valid address for business to opt-out or unsubscribe from your messages." [UK-1]
- Keep a list: "It makes good business sense for you to keep a ‘do not email or text’ list of any corporate subscribers." "You should screen any new B2B direct marketing lists against it." [UK-1]

**Sole traders and some partnerships.**
- "Because sole traders and some partnerships are treated by PECR as individual subscribers" "you can only market them by electronic mail if they have specifically consented, or the ‘soft opt-in’ applies." [UK-1]
- "You must not send marketing emails or texts to individuals without specific consent." [UK-2]
- The soft opt-in does not help with a stranger: "The term ‘soft opt-in’ is sometimes used to describe the rule about existing customers." [UK-2]
- Check first: the Companies House register shows whether a business is a company; GOV.UK says "You can get some details about a company for free" [UK-5].

**The UK GDPR, whenever a person is named.**
- "The UK GDPR still applies to B2B marketing if you are processing personal data." [UK-1]
- Lawful basis: "The two lawful bases most relevant in the B2B marketing context are consent and legitimate interests." "If PECR does not require consent, in many cases it is likely that legitimate interests will be the appropriate lawful basis." [UK-1] The ICO's three-part test: "identify a legitimate interest;" "show that the processing is necessary to achieve it; and" "balance it against the individual’s interests, rights and freedoms." [UK-1] The user writes this test down before the first send.
- Telling the person: if you collect personal data "from sources other than the individual (eg public sources or from third parties)", you must "provide privacy information within a reasonable period of obtaining the data", "no later than one month from the date of collection" [UK-1]. That month is the outer limit, not the whole rule. The UK GDPR, Art. 14(3)(b), sets an earlier deadline when you write to the person: "if the personal data are to be used for communication with the data subject," "at the latest at the time of the first communication to that data subject; or" (fragments) [UK-6]. This skill uses the details to write to the person, so in the UK the deadline is the first email, as in the EU.
- Objection: "This right is absolute and there are no grounds for you to refuse." [UK-1]
- Bought lists: "If you intend to buy or sell a list of business contacts for direct marketing purposes", "your use of any personal data on the list must comply with the UK GDPR." [UK-1]
- Suppress, do not just delete: "In most cases it is preferable to suppress their details." "Suppression involves keeping just enough information about someone to ensure you respect their preferences in the future." "By using a suppression list, you can check any new marketing lists against it." [UK-4]

**Not only email.** The ICO says electronic mail includes "direct messages via social media or any similar message that is stored electronically." [UK-1] So a LinkedIn message to a UK sole trader is treated like an email.

**Contact forms (UNVERIFIED).** Is a message typed into a business's website contact form "electronic mail" under PECR regulation 22 (or ePrivacy Article 13 in the EU)? No regulator page we found names contact forms, so the answer is UNVERIFIED (searched the ICO B2B and key-concepts pages, PECR regs 2 and 22, ePrivacy Art. 2, and the CJEU press release on Case C-102/20, 23 October 2026). This skill takes the cautious reading: for recipients who must consent first, a contact-form message is treated like an email, so no cold contact-form message to them. The reasoning, from mariem-compliance's review of this skill (draft, not a lawyer's opinion):
- The UK definition: "“electronic mail” means any text, voice, sound or image message sent over a public electronic communications network" "which can be stored in the network or in the recipient’s terminal equipment until it is collected by the recipient" (PECR reg. 2(1), fragments) [UK-8].
- The regulator's test: "Electronic mail is a private message stored for a specific intended recipient to collect." It lists, among other examples, "in-app messages; and" "direct messaging on social media (ie private messages)." It sets apart public adverts, which "are not stored for a specific intended recipient to collect." [UK-9]
- The EU definition is the same in substance: "‘electronic mail’ means any text, voice, sound or image message sent over a public communications network" (ePrivacy Art. 2(h), fragment) [EU-8]. In Case C-102/20 the Court of Justice's press release says "it is necessary to adopt an interpretation that is broad and evolving from a technological perspective of the type of communications covered by that directive", and "what matters is that there is a communication for a commercial purpose, which reaches, directly and individually, one or more email service users." (fragment) [EU-9]. The press release says it is "Unofficial document for media use, not binding on the Court of Justice."; the judgment text itself was not read (EUR-Lex returned an empty page).
- The reading: a contact-form message is private, written for one specific recipient, and stored until that recipient collects it, in the site's back end or in its mailbox. That fits the ICO's test. When the form delivers it as an email, the person filling in the form arguably instigates its transmission.
- Why it stays UNVERIFIED: (1) whether the message is "sent over a public electronic communications network" and stored "in the network" when the form sits on the recipient's own website; (2) whether the recipient is an "individual subscriber" for that message, since a "subscriber" is "a person who is a party to a contract with a provider of public electronic communications services" (PECR reg. 2(1), fragment) [UK-8].
- For people at companies (UK corporate subscribers) a contact form is a normal route, with the same "always" rules as an email.

**Not covered here (UK).** E-commerce rules on commercial communications: the Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002, regs 7 and 8 [UK-7]. They may require a commercial email to be clearly identifiable as commercial and to identify who it is sent for. Whether they apply depends on whether the user is a "service provider" under those regulations, which is a question for the user's adviser. This file does not state a duty; it points to the rule. The drafts in `SKILL.md` already say it is an offer and name the sender.

## European Union: ePrivacy Directive and the GDPR

Sources: Directive 2002/58/EC (ePrivacy), Article 13 [EU-1]; Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), Articles 14 and 21 and recital 47 [EU-2][EU-3][EU-4]; European Commission guidance [EU-5][EU-6]. The official texts are on EUR-Lex. On 23 October 2026 EUR-Lex returned an empty page to the tools used to write this file, so the quotes were read on legislation.gov.uk, the UK National Archives' copy of EU law: the ePrivacy Directive in its revised version (Article 13 as substituted in 2009) and the GDPR "as adopted" in 2016. Check the current EUR-Lex text before you rely on them.

**The email rule (ePrivacy Article 13).**
- 13(1): email for direct marketing "may be allowed only in respect of subscribers or users who have given their prior consent." (fragment) [EU-1]
- 13(2), the one exception: a business that "obtains from its customers their electronic contact details" (fragment) may use them "for direct marketing of its own similar products or services", "provided that customers clearly and distinctly are given the opportunity to object, free of charge and in an easy manner," (fragments) [EU-1]. It does not cover strangers.
- 13(4), always: marketing emails "which disguise or conceal the identity of the sender on whose behalf the communication is made," or "which do not have a valid address to which the recipient may send a request that such communications cease" are prohibited (fragments) [EU-1].
- 13(5): "Paragraphs 1 and 3 shall apply to subscribers who are natural persons." For other subscribers, Member States ensure "that the legitimate interests of subscribers other than natural persons with regard to unsolicited communications" "are sufficiently protected." (fragments) [EU-1]

In plain words: a directive is put into each country's own law, so the binding rule is national. Emailing a natural person (which usually includes a sole trader) needs prior consent, except your own existing customers. Emailing someone at a company is left to each country, and the answer differs. This file does not list countries. The user checks the data-protection or telecoms authority of the recipient's country.

**The person's data (GDPR).**
- Personal or not: the European Commission gives "a name and surname" and an address of the form name.surname at a company as examples of personal data, and "a company registration number" and an address of the form info@ at a company as examples of data that is not [EU-5].
- Telling them: when details did not come from the person, they must be told, among other things, "from which source the personal data originate, and if applicable, whether it came from publicly accessible sources;" (Art. 14(2)(f)) [EU-2], and, when the data is used to communicate with them, "at the latest at the time of the first communication to that data subject" (Art. 14(3)(b), fragment) [EU-2]. The information also covers who you are and how to reach you, the purpose and legal basis, the categories of data and the recipients (Art. 14(1)) [EU-2]. In practice: one short line in the email, and a link to a privacy notice with the rest.
- Objection: "Where personal data are processed for direct marketing purposes, the data subject shall have the right to object at any time" (Art. 21(2), fragment); then "the personal data shall no longer be processed for such purposes." (Art. 21(3), fragment) [EU-3]. The right must be "explicitly brought to the attention of the data subject" "and shall be presented clearly and separately from any other information." (Art. 21(4), fragments) [EU-3]. So the opt-out sits on its own line.
- Lawful basis: recital 47 says a legitimate interest "would need careful assessment including whether a data subject can reasonably expect at the time and in the context of the collection of the personal data that processing for that purpose may take place." [EU-4] Whether legitimate interests fit the user's case is a question for their adviser.
- Lists from someone else: the Commission says a company that receives a contact list "must be able to demonstrate that the data was obtained in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation", that where the list rests on consent, "the consent should've included the possibility to transmit the data to other recipients for their own direct marketing", and that it must "inform individuals, at the latest at the time of the first communication with them" (fragments) [EU-6]. It adds that marketing by email must also follow the ePrivacy rules [EU-6].
- Tracking pixels and tracked links: the European Data Protection Board's guidelines bring email tracking pixels and links within ePrivacy Article 5(3): "As such, Article 5(3) ePD is applicable, even if this storage is not permanent." [EU-7] Leave open and click tracking off for EU recipients unless the user has checked it.
- Senders outside the EU: whether the GDPR reaches a non-EU business sending B2B email into the EU is not settled here. Ask a lawyer.
- Not covered here: EU e-commerce rules on commercial communications (the EU counterpart of the UK regulations in [UK-7]). Not checked by this skill. They may require a commercial email to be clearly identifiable as such; ask an adviser.
- Contact forms: see "Contact forms (UNVERIFIED)" in the UK section; the same caution applies in the EU [EU-8][EU-9].

## Other countries

Not covered (for example Canada or Australia). Do not tell the user that email is allowed there. Ask them to read that country's regulator or law and tell you what it requires; draft only after they have, and label each draft "Rules for <country> not checked by this skill".

## Platforms and mailbox providers (terms, not law)

- **LinkedIn.** Its User Agreement, section 8.2, says you must not "Develop, support or use software, devices, scripts, robots or any other means or processes" "to scrape or copy the Services, including profiles and other data from the Services;" and must not "Use bots or other unauthorized automated methods to access the Services, add or download contacts" or "send or redirect messages" (fragments) [P-1]. So no collection from LinkedIn by an agent or a script, and no automated sending or connecting. The user writes and sends LinkedIn messages by hand.
- **Any other site.** Before copying anything from a directory, a marketplace or a member list, read its terms. If they forbid copying or automated collection, do not collect from it.
- **Gmail's sender guidelines** [P-2]: "Don't purchase email addresses from other companies." "Don't impersonate other domains or senders without permission." Subject lines must not start with "Re:" or "Fwd:" "unless the messages are actual replies or forwards". "Display names should never be used to attempt to deceive the recipient of the email."
